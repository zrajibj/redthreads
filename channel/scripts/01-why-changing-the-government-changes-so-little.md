# Red Threads · Video 1: Why does changing the government change so little?

Script v1 · 11 October 2026 · English master (the Bangla version is written from this by a native writer)

**Length:** about 640 words of narration, roughly 4:45 at 150 words a minute, plus a 20-second end screen.
**Format:** standard explainer in the TED-Ed style: one narrator, illustrated 2D animation, the red thread running through every scene.
**Rule for every frame:** an imaginary country. No real leaders, parties, flags or logos, and nothing that reads as a push against any government.

## 1. Narration only (for the voice actor)

Read warmly and clearly, like a favourite teacher telling a story. "/" marks a short pause. Leave real silence before the Luxemburg quote and before the last line.

**0:00** Imagine a country that has just changed its leaders. New faces on the posters, new names on the office doors, new promises on the news. / The next morning, a garment worker clocks in at the same factory, for the same wage, under the same owner.

**0:19** Something huge has changed. / And something even bigger has not.

**0:25** Countries all over the world know this feeling, and so does Bangladesh. Power changes hands, hope rises, and before long the same complaints return: corruption, prices, wages that don't keep up. / So why does changing the government change so little? / And what would it take to change the thing underneath?

**0:48** To answer that, it helps to picture a society as a building.

**0:54** At the top are the parts everyone can see: the government, the parliament, the courts, the parties, the newspapers and TV. This is where the arguments happen, and where the faces change.

**1:07** But every building stands on a foundation. In a society, the foundation is the economy: who owns the land, the factories and the money; who does the work; and who keeps what that work produces beyond what workers are paid.

**1:24** Karl Marx described society in just these terms: an economic base, with law and politics built on top of it. / His partner Friedrich Engels put it bluntly. The "ultimate causes of all social changes and political revolutions," he wrote, are found "not in men's brains", but in "changes in the modes of production and exchange".

**1:48** In other words: if you want to know why politics looks the way it does, look first at how wealth is made, and who ends up with it.

**2:00** Now watch what happens when the government changes. / The new leaders move into the top floor. But the building they move into stands on the same base. The same owners hold the same land and factories. The same workers do the same work. The surplus flows into the same locked room.

**2:23** Marx and Engels made the point sharply in the *Communist Manifesto*. "The executive of the modern State," they wrote, "is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie", the owning class. / Change the members of the committee, and the job description stays the same.

**2:44** Albert Einstein saw the same pattern in America. Private capital, he argued, grows into "an oligarchy", and political parties are "largely financed or otherwise influenced by private capitalists". Voters choose between candidates, but the money has often chosen first.

**3:00** So what about corruption? On this view, corruption isn't the disease. It's a symptom. / When land and wealth are concentrated in a few hands, the state becomes the biggest prize in the country. Whoever wins it faces the same pressures and the same temptations as whoever held it before.

**3:22** Does that mean politics doesn't matter? / Not at all. Rights, courts and a free press can protect people, and many scholars argue that strong institutions can reshape an economy. The question is what the change reaches.

**3:38** The revolutionary Rosa Luxemburg drew that line over a century ago. Change aimed only at the surface, she warned, gives you "the suppression of the abuses of capitalism instead of suppression of capitalism itself". / A fresh coat of paint, on the same foundation.

**3:57** So when we talk about change, the question isn't only "who should rule?" / It's also: who owns? Who works? Who keeps the surplus? And who decides?

**4:09** Those questions are harder than changing a portrait on a wall. People have answered them in different ways, some hopeful and some terrible, and Red Threads will look at both.

**4:22** But a society that only changes its faces will keep getting the same results. / To change what is built, you have to look at what it is built on.

**4:35** Next thread: why couldn't the most famous scientist in the world trust elections to stop the rich?
