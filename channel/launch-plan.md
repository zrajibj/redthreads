# Red Threads: Channel Launch Plan

YouTube, Facebook and Instagram · Draft 1 · 11 October 2026

## Summary

Red Threads should launch as an illustrated explainer channel that takes one question from everyday life (why rent is high, why work never gets shorter, why inequality exists at all) and follows the thread back to the socialist and anarchist thinkers in the study library. YouTube carries the full 4 to 6 minute animated lesson in the TED-Ed style; Instagram and Facebook carry vertical cut-downs and carousels that point back to it.

Start with one long video every two weeks and three short clips a week, and hold that pace for the first eight weeks before speeding up. The first lesson should be "Why did Einstein become a socialist?", built from his 1949 essay. It has the widest pull of any text in the library, it is short enough to source line by line, and it sets the channel's tone: curious, fair and sourced, never a rant. The full pilot script is in section 8.

One decision is still open: the language of the channel (section 9).

## 1. Positioning

**One line:** Red Threads follows the hidden threads that tie everyday life to the big ideas about who owns what, who works for whom, and how it could be different.

**Promise to the viewer:** in five minutes you will understand one idea well enough to explain it to a friend, and you will know exactly which book it came from.

**What makes it different from existing left channels:**

- **Questions, not verdicts.** Every video opens on a concrete question and earns its answer. The tone is a favourite teacher, not a pundit.
- **Primary sources on screen.** Each lesson is built on one text from the library and quotes it directly. The description lists the book, chapter and page.
- **Animation, not a talking head.** Illustrated 2D animation with one narrator, so the channel can grow without a presenter's face and can be dubbed into other languages cheaply.
- **The red thread itself.** A single red line runs through every video, drawing one idea into the next. It is the brand, the transition device and the thumbnail motif at once.

## 2. Audience

| Segment | Who they are | What they want from Red Threads |
|---|---|---|
| Primary: the curious and squeezed | 18 to 34, students and young workers, worried about rent, jobs and climate, not yet "political" | Plain answers to "why is it like this?" without jargon or tribal signalling |
| Secondary: the new reader | Has heard of Marx, Kropotkin or Fisher and wants a way in | A map of the books: what each one argues and where to start |
| Tertiary: teachers and study groups | Run reading circles or classes | Short, sourced videos to open a discussion, with questions attached |

Write for the primary segment. If a sentence only lands for someone who has already read the book, cut it or picture it.

## 3. Content pillars

Each pillar draws on specific books from Study Materials/Books. The Anarchist Library texts being added to Study Materials/Websites will feed pillar C once they land.

| Pillar | The question it answers | Core sources |
|---|---|---|
| A. How we got here | Where do money, the state, wages and inequality come from? | Varoufakis, *Talking to My Daughter About the Economy*; Engels, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*; Engels, *Principles of Communism* |
| B. Big ideas, explained | What did these thinkers actually say? | Marx and Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*; Einstein, *Why Socialism?*; Luxemburg, *Reform or Revolution?*; Jacobin, *The ABCs of Socialism* |
| C. Could it be otherwise? | What would another world look like, and why is it so hard to imagine? | Kropotkin, *The Conquest of Bread*; Fisher, *Capitalist Realism*; the utopian socialists in Engels |
| D. The history they skipped | What happened that the textbook left out? | Parenti, *Blackshirts and Reds*; the Owen story in Engels; the 1912 Socialist Party in the Jacobin introduction |

Rotate pillars so no two long videos in a row come from the same one.

## 4. Formats by platform

| Platform | Format | Spec | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | Animated lesson (TED-Ed style) | 16:9, 4 to 6 min, 550 to 900 words of narration | The flagship; everything else points here |
| YouTube Shorts | Cut-down clip | 9:16, 30 to 60 s | Discovery; links to the full lesson |
| Instagram Reels | Same cut-downs, recaptioned | 9:16, 30 to 60 s, burned-in captions | Discovery and shares |
| Instagram carousel | "The thread" | 6 to 8 slides: question, 4 to 5 beats, source, call to watch | Saves and shares; works without sound |
| Facebook Reels and Page | Cut-downs plus the full lesson uploaded natively | 9:16 clips; 16:9 full video | Older and South Asian audiences, groups, shares |
| YouTube Community / IG Stories | "Thread of the week" quote card, polls on the next topic | Static or 10 s animated | Keeps the channel active between lessons |

**Every long lesson yields:** one full video, three short clips (the hook, the single most surprising beat, the closing line), one carousel and one quote card. Plan the cut-downs at script stage by marking those three beats in the beat sheet, so they are animated to stand alone.

**What stays the same across platforms:** the narrator's voice, the palette, the red thread, and a source line on screen ("From: Einstein, *Why Socialism?*, 1949").

## 5. Posting cadence

**Weeks 1 to 8 (launch phase).** Bank three finished lessons before going public so the schedule survives a slow week.

| Day | Post |
|---|---|
| Every other Tuesday | Long lesson on YouTube and Facebook |
| Wednesday, Friday, Sunday | One short clip on Shorts, Reels and Facebook Reels |
| Thursday | Carousel on Instagram and Facebook |
| Monday | Quote card or poll for the next topic |

**Weeks 9 onward.** If the shorts are pulling viewers into the long lessons, move to one lesson a week. If the long lessons are slow to make, keep the fortnightly lesson and add a cheaper second format: a 2 to 3 minute "One Page" video that animates a single paragraph from a book with the quote on screen.

**What to measure for the first eight weeks:** average view duration on the long lesson (aim for over 50%), the share of Shorts and Reels viewers who click through to the long lesson, saves on carousels, and which pillar draws the most returning viewers. Let those numbers pick the next eight topics.

## 6. Branding basics

**Name and handles.** Red Threads on every platform. Check availability of the same handle everywhere before launch (for example @redthreads, @redthreads.tv or @redthreadsexplained) and use the one that is free on all three.

**Logo.** A wordmark in which a single red thread loops through the letters and forms the crossbar of the T. The same thread, on its own, is the profile icon.

**Palette.** Warm paper and ink with one red accent, following the skill's rule of one accent colour that guides the eye.

| Role | Colour | Hex |
|---|---|---|
| Accent (the thread) | Thread red | #C8102E |
| Background | Paper cream | #F2E8D5 |
| Ink and outlines | Ink | #1E1E24 |
| Secondary, cool | Deep teal | #2F6F73 |
| Secondary, warm | Ochre | #D9A441 |
| Neutral | Slate | #6B6B73 |

Each lesson may add two or three colours for its subject (for example soot greys for an industrial-revolution story), but the red stays reserved for the thread and the one thing that matters in each shot.

**Type.** A characterful serif for titles and quotes (for example Fraunces) and a clean sans for labels and captions (for example Inter). Both are free Google Fonts.

**Texture.** Light paper grain and a subtle halftone over flat fills, so the frames feel printed, like a pamphlet.

**Sound.** A short sting of a string being pulled taut and plucked, used on the end card only. Underscore follows the skill: soft piano, plucked strings, light percussion, always well under the voice.

**End card.** "Lesson by Red Threads · Directed by [animator] · Narrated by [VO]", the source text, and links to the next lesson and the related book.

**Thumbnails.** One illustrated frame with one subject and the red thread, readable at phone size, at most three or four words.

## 7. First batch: ten episodes

Each idea names its source, the skill format it uses, and why it should travel. Episode 1 is the pilot.

**1. Why did Einstein become a socialist?** · Pillar B · Standard explainer
Source: Albert Einstein, "Why Socialism?", *Monthly Review*, May 1949.
In the first issue of a small magazine, the world's most famous scientist argues that "the economic anarchy of capitalist society" is "the real source of the evil", then warns that a planned economy can bring "the complete enslavement of the individual". A famous name, a short source and a real twist. Full script in section 8.

**2. Why didn't Aboriginal Australians invade Britain?** · Pillar A · Standard explainer
Source: Yanis Varoufakis, *Talking to My Daughter About the Economy*, chapter 1, "Why So Much Inequality?".
Varoufakis rejects "Europeans were smarter" and traces the answer to agricultural surplus, which he says gave birth to "writing, debt, money, states, bureaucracy, armies, clergy, technology". A provocative question with a respectful, anti-racist answer. Handle with care: consult Aboriginal sources and avoid stereotyped imagery.

**3. The factory owner who called his workers "slaves at my mercy"** · Pillar D · History and biography
Source: Engels, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, chapter 1 (Robert Owen at New Lanark).
From 1800 Owen ran a Scottish cotton mill of 2,500 people on a 10 and a half hour day while rivals worked 13 to 14, paid full wages through a four-month shutdown, and still doubled the business. Then he asked where the extra wealth went. A hero story that turns into a question.

**4. Why is it easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism?** · Pillar C · Standard explainer
Source: Mark Fisher, *Capitalist Realism*, chapter 1.
Fisher opens on the film *Children of Men* and the line he attributes to Fredric Jameson and Slavoj Žižek. Strong for an audience raised on dystopias; the visual metaphor is a world painted on the inside of a box.

**5. Could everyone work four or five hours a day?** · Pillar C · "Is it ___?" myth-buster
Source: Peter Kropotkin, *The Conquest of Bread*, chapters on "Well-Being for All" and on work hours.
Kropotkin cites Benjamin Franklin's five-hour estimate and argues that "well-being for all is not a dream". Pairs naturally with today's four-day-week debate; state clearly what has and has not changed since 1892.

**6. On Trial: can you vote your way out of capitalism?** · Pillar B · Debate and trial (own name, not "History vs.")
Source: Rosa Luxemburg, *Reform or Revolution?* (1899, revised 1908).
Eduard Bernstein argues that capitalism is adapting and reforms will add up to socialism; Luxemburg answers chapter by chapter. Two voices, the viewer decides. Present Bernstein's case at full strength.

**7. The lost first draft of the Communist Manifesto** · Pillar B · "The surprising history of ___"
Source: Engels, *Principles of Communism* (1847), the question-and-answer draft that preceded the *Manifesto*.
Written as a catechism of numbered questions ("What is Communism?", "What is the proletariat?", "Has there then not always been a proletariat?"). The Q&A form is ready-made for animation and for a carousel.

**8. "A spectre is haunting Europe": how a pamphlet outlived its century** · Pillar B · History and biography
Source: Marx and Engels, *The Communist Manifesto* (Penguin Classics edition with Gareth Stedman Jones's introduction).
What the Manifesto said in 1848, who read it at the time (very few), and why it came back. Use the introduction for the publishing history and keep every number sourced.

**9. Why did big business back Mussolini and Hitler?** · Pillar D · Standard explainer
Source: Michael Parenti, *Blackshirts and Reds*, chapter 1, "Rational Fascism" ("Plutocrats Choose Autocrats", "Whom Did the Fascists Support?").
Parenti argues that fascism used "irrational mass appeals" for "the rational ends of class domination". The most contested claims in the batch: cross-check each one against mainstream historians and say where they differ.

**10. Is the public library socialism?** · Pillar B · Myth-buster
Source: *The ABCs of Socialism* (Jacobin), Chris Maisano, "Isn't America already kind of socialist?".
Starts from the viral meme that lists fire departments and libraries as "socialism" and asks what the word actually means. A friendly entry point and good Shorts material. Frame it for a global audience, not only an American one.

**Order for the first eight weeks:** 1 (pilot), 3, 4, 2, 10, 5, 7, 6. Hold 8 and 9 until the channel has a reputation for careful sourcing, because they draw the most pushback.

## 8. Pilot script

Written with the ted-ed-style-explainer skill. Every quotation is from Einstein's essay as printed in the PDF in Study Materials/Books. Pause beats are marked "/", emphasis with *asterisks*.

**TITLE OPTIONS:** Why did Einstein become a socialist? · Einstein's forgotten essay on money and loneliness · What Einstein thought was wrong with the economy
**FORMAT:** Standard explainer | **TARGET LENGTH:** about 5 min / 780 words
**HOOK IMAGE:** a printing press in a cramped New York office; a red thread runs off the press and ends in a signature: A. Einstein.

**[Beat 1 | 0:00]** May, 1949. / In a cramped office in New York, a few editors are putting together the first issue of a new magazine. It has no money, no famous name, and very few readers. / But the opening essay is signed by the most famous scientist on Earth.
VISUAL: Pages slide off a press. The red thread rides out on the paper and loops into a signature.
TRANSITION: Push into the signature; the ink lines become Einstein's hair.
SOUND: Press clatter, then quiet.

**[Beat 2 | 0:14]** Albert Einstein. The man who showed that time can stretch and space can bend. / And his essay's title is a question: *Why Socialism?*
VISUAL: Einstein, drawn simply, at a desk; clocks warp behind him, then settle as he writes the title.
TRANSITION: The title's letters drop into a stack of 1949 newspapers.

**[Beat 3 | 0:24]** In America in 1949, that was a risky thing to sign. / So why would a physicist, with plenty to lose, put his name to it? And what did he think was actually wrong?
VISUAL: Newspaper headlines about the Cold War blur past; Einstein's figure stands still among them.
SOUND: Music drops to a single plucked note.

**[Beat 4 | 0:38]** Einstein begins by asking whether he has any right to speak. / Economics, he says, is not like astronomy. Its facts are tangled by too many causes. And most of recorded history belongs to what the economist Thorstein Veblen called "the predatory phase": conquerors seizing the land and turning themselves into a ruling class.
VISUAL: Planets on clean orbits; then a tangle of threads. Armoured figures plant flags on fields while farmers bow.
TRANSITION: The flags become fence posts.

**[Beat 5 | 1:02]** Laws drawn from that phase, he argues, cannot tell us much about what comes after it. And science can supply the means to an end, but it cannot choose the end. / So experts are not the only ones with a right to speak about how society is organized.
VISUAL: A scientist's toolbox opens; tools lift out, but a compass needle inside spins, pointing nowhere.

**[Beat 6 | 1:22]** Then he tells a story. A visitor, "intelligent and well-disposed", listens to Einstein's fear of another war, and calmly asks: / "Why are you so deeply opposed to the disappearance of the human race?"
VISUAL: Two figures in armchairs. The visitor's teacup is perfectly still. Einstein's coffee ripples.
SOUND: A clock ticking; nothing else.

**[Beat 7 | 1:40]** Einstein heard that question as a symptom of something wider: "a painful solitude and isolation" that, he said, so many people were suffering from. / What causes it? / Is there a way out?
VISUAL: Pull back from the room to a city of lit windows, each with a single figure inside.
TRANSITION: One window grows to fill the screen.

**[Beat 8 | 1:58]** His answer starts with a simple observation. We are, at the same time, solitary beings and social beings. We look after ourselves, and we want the recognition and affection of others.
VISUAL: A figure split down the middle: one half guards a small fire, the other half reaches out a hand.

**[Beat 9 | 2:12]** And everything we have comes through other people. Society, he writes, gives us "food, clothing, a home, the tools of work, language, the forms of thought, and most of the content of thought."
VISUAL: The core metaphor. One person stands alone on a blank page. The camera pulls back and red threads appear from their coat, their bread, their words, running out to farmers, miners, teachers and the long line of people who lived before.
TRANSITION: The threads tighten into a web that fills the frame.
CUT-DOWN: Clip 2 (the most surprising beat).

**[Beat 10 | 2:34]** Unlike ants and bees, whose lives are fixed by instinct, our way of living together can change. That, he says, is where hope lies.
VISUAL: An anthill frozen in place; beside it, a human town that rebuilds itself in fast motion.

**[Beat 11 | 2:46]** But here is the crisis, as Einstein saw it. We feel our dependence on others not as a protective force, but as a threat. Our selfish drives are constantly encouraged; our social ones wither. We become, in his words, "prisoners of their own egotism".
VISUAL: The web from Beat 9 is still there, but each figure now holds a pair of scissors, eyeing the threads.

**[Beat 12 | 3:06]** The source of that, he argues, is "the economic anarchy of capitalist society": a huge community of producers, each trying to deprive the others of the fruits of their shared work, "not by force, but on the whole in faithful compliance with legally established rules."
VISUAL: A long shared table of produce; hands reach in from all sides and pull the food toward themselves, each holding a stamped legal document.

**[Beat 13 | 3:28]** Look at the wage. A worker is not paid the value of what they produce. They are paid according to their minimum needs, and how many others are competing for the same job.
VISUAL: A baker pulls a whole loaf from an oven and is handed one slice. Behind them, a queue of job seekers grows, and the slice gets thinner.

**[Beat 14 | 3:44]** Over time, capital gathers into fewer hands, until it forms what Einstein called "an oligarchy of private capital", powerful enough to influence political parties, and the press, radio and schools that shape what citizens know.
VISUAL: The threads of the web slide toward a single knot held by a few hands; the knot pulls on a ballot box, a radio and a school bell.

**[Beat 15 | 4:02]** The worst of it, he wrote, is "this crippling of individuals". Schools train students to "worship acquisitive success".
VISUAL: Pupils at desks race to climb a ladder of coins; one helps another up and is told off.

**[Beat 16 | 4:14]** His remedy: an economy owned by society and planned for need, that guarantees "a livelihood to every man, woman, and child", with schools that teach responsibility for one another.
VISUAL: The knot loosens; threads spread back out across the table, and every place is set.
SOUND: Music lifts.

**[Beat 17 | 4:30]** And then Einstein does something unusual for an argument like this. / He warns against his own answer. "A planned economy is not yet socialism," he writes. It could come with "the complete enslavement of the individual."
VISUAL: Freeze. The orderly planned city from Beat 16 tilts; its threads turn into bars.
SOUND: Music cuts to silence before the quote.

**[Beat 18 | 4:46]** How do you stop a central bureaucracy from becoming all-powerful? How do you protect the rights of the individual? / He does not claim to know. He leaves those questions open, and asks for them to be discussed freely, at a time when, he wrote, such discussion had "come under a powerful taboo."
VISUAL: The bars bend back into threads; question marks hang on them like knots.

**[Beat 19 | 5:06]** In the end, Einstein's diagnosis was not really about money. It was about loneliness. / "Man can find meaning in life, short and perilous as it is," he wrote, "only through devoting himself to society."
VISUAL: Return to the lit windows from Beat 7. One by one, red threads run between them.

**[Beat 20 | 5:22]** He spent his life searching for the hidden laws that connect the universe. / In this essay, he was looking for the threads that connect us.
VISUAL: Pull back until the city's threads become a single red line that writes "Red Threads".
SOUND: The plucked-string sting.
CUT-DOWN: Clip 3 (the closing line). Clip 1 is Beats 1 to 3.

**CREDITS:** Lesson by Red Threads · Directed by [animator] · Narrated by [VO] · Music by [composer]

**SOURCES**

- Albert Einstein, "Why Socialism?", *Monthly Review* vol. 1 no. 1, May 1949 (reprinted by Monthly Review, May 2009). PDF in Study Materials/Books. All quotations are from this text.
- Beat 3 ("a risky thing to sign") is context, not from the essay. Before release, confirm it with an outside source, for example the FBI's released file on Einstein in the FBI Vault, and add that source to the description.

**THINK QUESTIONS**

1. Why does Einstein say experts should not be the only ones allowed to speak about how society is organized?
2. What does Einstein mean when he says a worker is not paid "the value of his product"?
3. Why does Einstein say a planned economy is "not yet socialism"?
4. Einstein links an economic system to loneliness. Do you find that convincing? Why or why not?

**DISCUSS:** Einstein said science can supply the means but not the ends. Who should decide the ends?

## 9. Decision needed: language

All ten source texts are in English. The plan above assumes English narration. Three options:

- **English only (recommended to start).** Widest reach, quotes stay exact, one voiceover per lesson. Add Bangla subtitles from the first lesson.
- **Bangla only.** A much less crowded niche with a strong home audience, but quotes must be translated and checked, and reach outside Bangladesh and India is smaller.
- **Both.** English master with a Bangla dub of each lesson, posted as a separate upload or as a YouTube multi-language audio track. Roughly double the voice and caption work, but the animation is shared, which is where animated, no-presenter lessons pay off.

## 10. Production workflow per lesson

1. **Pitch** in one sentence: the question, why it surprises, the source text and page.
2. **Script** with the ted-ed-style-explainer skill: 550 to 900 words, quotes checked against the PDF, three cut-down beats marked.
3. **Beat sheet** with narration, visual, transition, on-screen text and sound per beat.
4. **Scratch VO**, then a timed animatic.
5. **Style frames** in the channel palette, three to five per lesson.
6. **Final VO, animation, music and sound.**
7. **Fact check** by someone who has read the source, and a clarity check by someone who has not.
8. **Package:** 16:9 master, three 9:16 cut-downs, captions file, thumbnail, carousel, description with sources and think questions.

## 11. Risks and how to handle them

- **Reach limits on political content.** Meta says Instagram and Threads do not proactively recommend political content to people who have not opted in, and YouTube applies advertiser-friendly rules to controversial topics. Frame titles as questions about history and economics, avoid party politics and current candidates, and lean on shares, saves and search rather than recommendations.
- **Accuracy attacks.** This genre is heavily fact-checked by critics. Quote exactly, link every source, mark inference as inference, and pin a correction comment when something is wrong.
- **Copyright.** Quote short passages only. Never show scanned pages or the Jacobin book's illustrations. The Foreign Languages Press editions of Luxemburg and Engels in the library are Creative Commons; the Engels edition is non-commercial (CC BY-NC-SA), so a monetized channel should quote rather than reproduce it, or use a public-domain translation.
- **Fairness.** Give the strongest version of the opposing view in each lesson, especially in the debate format and in episodes 2 and 9. It builds trust with the curious viewers the channel is for.
