# Red Threads: Channel Launch Plan

YouTube, Facebook and Instagram · Draft 3 · 11 October 2026

## Summary

Red Threads exists to answer one question for people everywhere: **why does changing the government change so little?** In country after country, from Bangladesh to Britain, power changes hands, and each time hope tends to turn into the same complaints about corruption and the same lives for the people who do the work. The channel's argument, drawn from the books in the study library, is that the problem sits in the base of society (who owns the land, the factories and the wealth, who works, and who keeps the surplus), and that politics, parties and even corruption are built on top of that base. Change the faces at the top without changing the base, and the building stays the same.

Every video takes one everyday experience (a wage that never rises, a bribe at an office, a new party making old promises) and follows the red thread down to the base, using one book as the guide. YouTube and Facebook carry the full 4 to 6 minute animated lesson in the TED-Ed style; Reels, Shorts and carousels carry cut-downs that pull viewers to it.

The pilot is "Why does changing the government change so little?" (section 8a). Two decisions are open: the language (section 10) and whether you are happy with the goal as written below.

## Goal

**Help people around the world see the base beneath the politics, so that the next time they demand change, they demand change to the base and not only to the faces in power.**

That makes the channel educational, not partisan, and never a campaign against the government. It never tells viewers which party to support, never attacks the current government or any past one, and never calls people onto the streets. It explains the structure that every government inherits. This keeps the channel credible, keeps it within platform rules on political content, and lowers legal risk (section 12).

**Targets for the first six months:**

- **Understanding:** more than half of viewers watch the long lessons to the end.
- **Reach:** 1,000 YouTube subscribers and a growing Facebook following, which also meets YouTube's subscriber requirement for monetization.
- **Conversion:** a steady share of Reels and Shorts viewers clicking through to the full lesson, and subscribers gained per 1,000 views rising lesson on lesson (section 9).
- **Depth:** comments that use the channel's own words, such as "it's the base, not the face", and questions about the books.

## 1. Positioning

**One line:** Red Threads shows what is underneath the government: the base of who owns, who works and who keeps the surplus, which stays the same when the rulers change.

**Promise to the viewer:** in five minutes you will understand one reason why things stay the same after a change in power, wherever you live, and you will know which book it comes from.

**What makes it different:**

- **The base, not the face.** Most political content, in Bangladesh and everywhere else, is about personalities and parties. Red Threads is about the structure every government inherits. The channel names systems, not villains, and never takes aim at the government of the day.
- **Questions, not slogans.** Every video opens on a concrete question and earns its answer. The tone is a favourite teacher, not a speaker at a rally.
- **Primary sources on screen.** Each lesson quotes one text from the library and lists the book, chapter and page in the description.
- **Animation, not a talking head.** One narrator and illustrated 2D animation. No presenter's face, which makes dubbing easy and keeps the focus on ideas, not on a personality.
- **The red thread.** A single red line runs through every video, from the surface of daily life down to the base. It is the brand, the transition device and the thumbnail motif at once.

## 2. Audience

| Segment | Who they are | What they want from Red Threads |
|---|---|---|

| Secondary: workers and their families | Garment, factory, transport and gig workers, and the families who depend on them | Their own lives explained: wages, prices, debt, who profits |
| Tertiary: viewers who follow Bangladesh and South Asia | The Bangladeshi diaspora and viewers across South Asia | Context on the region, told through ideas that apply everywhere |

Write for the primary segment. If a sentence only lands for someone who has already read the book, cut it or picture it.

## 3. The core idea, in plain words

Every lesson returns to one picture: **society as a building.**

- **The base** is how a society produces and shares its wealth: who owns the land, factories and capital, who works for them, and who keeps the surplus that work creates.
- **The building on top** is the government, the laws, the courts, the parties, the media, and even the ideas people take as common sense.

Marx described society this way, as an economic base with a legal and political superstructure on top, in the preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859). The books in the library carry the same thread:

- **Engels** writes that the "ultimate causes of all social changes and political revolutions are to be sought, not in men's brains", but "in changes in the modes of production and exchange". (*Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, chapter 3)
- **The Communist Manifesto** says "the executive of the modern State is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie": whoever sits in the chair, the chair serves the owners.
- **Einstein** argues that private capital becomes "an oligarchy" whose power "cannot be effectively checked even by a democratically organized political society", because parties are "largely financed or otherwise influenced by private capitalists". (*Why Socialism?*, 1949)
- **Rosa Luxemburg** warns that working only on the surface gives you "the suppression of the abuses of capitalism instead of suppression of capitalism itself". (*Reform or Revolution?*, chapter 8)

**How corruption fits:** the channel treats corruption as a symptom, not the disease. When wealth and land are concentrated, the state becomes the biggest prize, and whoever wins it is pulled by the same incentives as whoever held it before. New faces sit in the same chair and the same people pay.

**Fairness rule:** many scholars argue that institutions (courts, a free press, election rules) also shape the economy, and that the building can change the base. Lessons say so where it matters, and let viewers weigh the arguments.

## 5. Formats by platform

YouTube is the global home of long explainers, so it leads. Facebook is the largest social platform in Bangladesh and many other countries, so it carries the full lesson too, not just reposts.

| Platform | Format | Spec | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | Animated lesson (TED-Ed style) | 16:9, 4 to 6 min, 550 to 900 words of narration | The flagship for the global audience; search, recommendations and later monetization |
| Facebook Page | The same full lesson, uploaded natively | 16:9 with burned-in captions | Bangladesh and other Facebook-first countries; shares into groups |
| Facebook Reels, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts | Cut-down clips | 9:16, 30 to 60 s, burned-in captions | Discovery; each points to the full lesson |
| Instagram and Facebook carousels | "The thread" | 6 to 8 slides: question, 4 to 5 beats, source, call to watch | Saves and shares; works without sound |
| YouTube Community, IG and FB Stories | "Base, not face" quote card or poll on the next topic | Static or 10 s animated | Keeps the channel active between lessons |

**Every long lesson yields:** one full video, three short clips (the hook, the single most surprising beat, the closing line), one carousel and one quote card. Mark those three beats in the script so they are animated to stand alone.

**What stays the same across platforms:** the narrator's voice, the palette, the red thread, and a source line on screen ("From: Engels, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*").

## 6. Posting cadence

**Weeks 1 to 8 (launch phase).** Bank three finished lessons before going public so the schedule survives a slow week.

| Day | Post |
|---|---|
| Every other Tuesday | Long lesson on YouTube and Facebook |
| Wednesday, Friday, Sunday | One short clip on Facebook Reels, Instagram Reels and Shorts |
| Thursday | Carousel on Instagram and Facebook |
| Monday | Quote card or poll for the next topic |

**Weeks 9 onward.** If the clips are pulling viewers into the long lessons, move to one lesson a week. If the lessons are slow to make, keep the fortnightly lesson and add a cheaper "One Page" format: 2 to 3 minutes animating a single paragraph from a book, with the quote on screen.

**News moments.** When something happens that shows the base at work (a budget, a wage decision, a price rise, a factory disaster), post a short clip within days that links it to an existing lesson. Explain the structure behind the event; never attack the government, a party or individuals.

## 7. Branding basics

**Name and handles.** Red Threads on every platform. Check the same handle everywhere before launch (for example @redthreads, @redthreads.tv or @redthreadsbd) and use the one that is free on all three.

**Tagline.** "The base, not the face." In Bangla, a short equivalent such as "মুখ নয়, ভিত্তি" (not the face, the foundation); have a native writer settle the wording.

**Logo.** A wordmark in which a single red thread loops through the letters and forms the crossbar of the T. The same thread on its own is the profile icon.

**Palette.** Warm paper and ink with one red accent that guides the eye.

| Role | Colour | Hex |
|---|---|---|
| Accent (the thread) | Thread red | #C8102E |
| Background | Paper cream | #F2E8D5 |
| Ink and outlines | Ink | #1E1E24 |
| Secondary, cool | Deep teal | #2F6F73 |
| Secondary, warm | Ochre | #D9A441 |
| Neutral | Slate | #6B6B73 |

The red stays reserved for the thread and for the one thing that matters in each shot. Avoid party colours as main colours, so the channel never reads as belonging to a party.

**Type.** A characterful serif for English titles (for example Fraunces), a clean sans for labels (for example Inter), and a Bangla typeface that pairs with them (for example Hind Siliguri or Noto Serif Bengali, both free Google Fonts).

**Texture.** Light paper grain and a subtle halftone over flat fills, so frames feel printed, like a pamphlet.

**Sound.** A short sting of a string pulled taut and plucked, on the end card only. Underscore follows the skill: soft piano, plucked strings, light percussion, always well under the voice. Instruments from the place a lesson is about, such as the dotara or ektara for a Bangladesh story, give each lesson local colour.

**End card and thumbnails.** End card: "Lesson by Red Threads · Directed by [animator] · Narrated by [VO]", the source text, and the next lesson. Thumbnails: one illustrated frame, one subject, the red thread, at most three or four words.

## 8. First batch: ten episodes

Each idea names its source, the skill format it uses, and a hook that works anywhere, with a Bangladesh example where it fits. Episode 1 is the pilot; its full script is in section 8a.

**1. Why does changing the government change so little?** · Pillar B · Standard explainer
Source: Engels, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, chapter 3; *The Communist Manifesto*; Luxemburg, *Reform or Revolution?*, chapter 8.
The channel's thesis in one lesson: society as a building, and why new tenants on the top floor do not change the foundation.

**2. Why can't elections stop the rich?** · Pillar B · Standard explainer
Source: Einstein, "Why Socialism?", *Monthly Review*, May 1949.
The world's most famous scientist argues that parties "largely financed or otherwise influenced by private capitalists" separate voters from lawmakers, then warns that a planned economy can bring "the complete enslavement of the individual". Hook: election promises versus what any winner can actually change. The full script is in Appendix A.

**3. The good boss who called his workers "slaves at my mercy"** · Pillar B · History and biography
Source: Engels, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, chapter 1 (Robert Owen at New Lanark).
Owen ran his Scottish mill on a 10 and a half hour day while rivals worked 13 to 14, paid full wages through a four-month shutdown, and still doubled the business. Yet he concluded his workers were "slaves at my mercy". The lesson: even a good person at the top can't fix the base. Hook: the idea, heard in every country, that an honest leader is all we need.

**4. Where does power come from?** · Pillar A · Standard explainer
Source: Varoufakis, *Talking to My Daughter About the Economy*, chapter 1.
Varoufakis traces writing, debt, money, the state and armies back to agricultural surplus. Whoever controls the surplus builds the state. Hook: the fields and factories of any country, from Bangladesh's garment belt to Silicon Valley, and who keeps what they produce.

**5. Who does the state work for?** · Pillar B · Standard explainer
Source: *The Communist Manifesto*, section 1 ("a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie").
What the Manifesto meant, where it holds and where critics say it goes too far. Hook: why the same business interests sit close to every government.

**6. Can a new government fix a broken system?** · Pillar B · Debate (the channel's own "On Trial" format)
Source: Luxemburg, *Reform or Revolution?* (1899, revised 1908).
Eduard Bernstein argues that reforms will add up to a new society; Luxemburg answers that they only reach "surface modifications of the old society". Two voices, the viewer decides. Present Bernstein's case at full strength, and keep the lesson about ideas, not about any call to action.

**7. Why is it easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism?** · Pillar C · Standard explainer
Source: Fisher, *Capitalist Realism*, chapter 1.
Fisher's "capitalist realism" explains why the only change people can picture is a change of faces. Hook: "there is no alternative" as the common sense of every party.

**8. What is a class, and which one are you in?** · Pillar A · "The surprising history of ___"
Source: Engels, *Principles of Communism* (1847), the question-and-answer draft of the Manifesto ("What is the proletariat?", "Has there then not always been a proletariat?").
The Q&A form suits animation and carousels. Hook: the student, the garment worker, the delivery rider and the software tester, and what they share.

**9. Doesn't changing the base always end in dictatorship?** · Pillar D · Myth-buster
Source: *The ABCs of Socialism* (Jacobin), "Doesn't socialism always end up in dictatorship?"; Einstein's own warning about bureaucracy.
The objection every viewer will raise, answered honestly, including what went wrong in the twentieth century. This lesson earns the channel's credibility.

**10. What would a different base look like?** · Pillar D · Standard explainer
Source: Kropotkin, *The Conquest of Bread*, "Well-Being for All".
Kropotkin argues that "well-being for all is not a dream" and that a few hours of work a day could provide for everyone. Ends the batch on what is possible, not only on what is wrong.

**Order for the first eight weeks:** 1 (pilot), 3, 2, 4, 7, 6, 8, 9. Hold 5 and 10 for the second batch.

## 8a. Pilot script: Why does changing the government change so little?

Written with the ted-ed-style-explainer skill. Quotations are from the PDFs in Study Materials/Books unless the sources list says otherwise. Pause beats are marked "/". The English script is the master; the Bangla version should be written from it by a native writer, not machine-translated.

**TITLE OPTIONS:** Why does changing the government change so little? · The problem isn't who rules. It's what they stand on · সরকার বদলায়, ব্যবস্থা বদলায় না কেন?
**FORMAT:** Standard explainer | **TARGET LENGTH:** about 5 min / 760 words
**HOOK IMAGE:** a nameplate on an office door being unscrewed and replaced; a single red thread runs from the door down into the ground.

**[Beat 1 | 0:00]** Imagine a country that has just changed its leaders. New faces on the posters, new names on the office doors, new promises on the news. / The next morning, a garment worker clocks in at the same factory, for the same wage, under the same owner.
VISUAL: An imaginary city: a nameplate unscrewed and replaced, posters pasted over posters, a TV anchor's lips moving. Cut to dawn: a factory gate, a queue of workers, the same red thread now tied to a time card. No real leaders, parties or flags.
SOUND: Busy news chatter, then a factory bell and silence.

**[Beat 2 | 0:16]** Something huge has changed. / And something even bigger has not.
VISUAL: Split frame: fresh posters on one side, the sewing floor unchanged on the other.
CUT-DOWN: Clip 1 is Beats 1 to 3.

**[Beat 3 | 0:22]** Countries all over the world know this feeling, and so does Bangladesh. Power changes hands, hope rises, and before long the same complaints return: corruption, prices, wages that don't keep up. / So why does changing the government change so little? / And what would it take to change the thing underneath?
VISUAL: A calendar flips through the decades; the same street stall, the same price board, the same worried faces in each.
SOUND: Music drops to a single plucked note.

**[Beat 4 | 0:44]** To answer that, it helps to picture a society as a building.
VISUAL: The red thread draws the outline of a tall building on a blank page.

**[Beat 5 | 0:50]** At the top are the parts everyone can see: the government, the parliament, the courts, the parties, the newspapers and TV. This is where the arguments happen, and where the faces change.
VISUAL: Floors fill in: a parliament chamber, a courtroom, TV screens, party banners. Tiny figures swap places in the top-floor office.

**[Beat 6 | 1:06]** But every building stands on a foundation. In a society, the foundation is the economy: who owns the land, the factories and the money; who does the work; and who keeps what that work produces beyond what workers are paid.
VISUAL: The camera sinks through the floors to the basement. Fields, factories and a bank vault sit in the foundation. Workers carry bricks of value up the stairs; most of the bricks go into a locked room.

**[Beat 7 | 1:28]** Karl Marx described society in just these terms: an economic base, with law and politics built on top of it. / His partner Friedrich Engels put it bluntly. The "ultimate causes of all social changes and political revolutions," he wrote, are found "not in men's brains", but in "changes in the modes of production and exchange".
VISUAL: Two bearded figures sketching the building on a tavern table. Engels' words appear briefly as hand-lettered text along the red thread.

**[Beat 8 | 1:52]** In other words: if you want to know why politics looks the way it does, look first at how wealth is made, and who ends up with it.
VISUAL: A magnifying glass passes over the top floor and finds nothing; moved down to the foundation, it lights up the locked room.

**[Beat 9 | 2:04]** Now watch what happens when the government changes. / The new leaders move into the top floor. But the building they move into stands on the same base. The same owners hold the same land and factories. The same workers do the same work. The surplus flows into the same locked room.
VISUAL: Movers carry out the old leader's nameplate and screw on a new one. The camera pans down: in the foundation, nothing has moved.
CUT-DOWN: Clip 2 (the most surprising beat).

**[Beat 10 | 2:28]** Marx and Engels made the point sharply in the *Communist Manifesto*. "The executive of the modern State," they wrote, "is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie", the owning class. / Change the members of the committee, and the job description stays the same.
VISUAL: A boardroom table on the top floor. Figures rotate through the chairs; the agenda on the table never changes.

**[Beat 11 | 2:50]** Albert Einstein saw the same pattern in America. Private capital, he argued, grows into "an oligarchy", and political parties are "largely financed or otherwise influenced by private capitalists". Voters choose between candidates, but the money has often chosen first.
VISUAL: A ballot box; threads run from its slot not to voters but to a few hands holding purses.

**[Beat 12 | 3:12]** So what about corruption? On this view, corruption isn't the disease. It's a symptom. / When land and wealth are concentrated in a few hands, the state becomes the biggest prize in the country. Whoever wins it faces the same pressures and the same temptations as whoever held it before.
VISUAL: The top-floor office becomes a trophy on a pedestal; different hands grab for it in turn, and each pair of hands is pulled by the same threads from the basement.

**[Beat 13 | 3:36]** Does that mean politics doesn't matter? / Not at all. Rights, courts and a free press can protect people, and many scholars argue that strong institutions can reshape an economy. The question is what the change reaches.
VISUAL: The building's windows light up one by one as rights are won. The camera tilts down: the foundation is still in shadow.

**[Beat 14 | 3:54]** The revolutionary Rosa Luxemburg drew that line over a century ago. Change aimed only at the surface, she warned, gives you "the suppression of the abuses of capitalism instead of suppression of capitalism itself". / A fresh coat of paint, on the same foundation.
VISUAL: Painters cover a cracked wall in bright colours. A crack runs up through the new paint.
SUBSCRIBE PROMPT: after this line, the 3-second red-thread knot appears in a corner, silently.

**[Beat 15 | 4:16]** So when we talk about change, the question isn't only "who should rule?" / It's also: who owns? Who works? Who keeps the surplus? And who decides?
VISUAL: The four questions hang like knots on the red thread, which runs from the top floor down into the foundation.

**[Beat 16 | 4:32]** Those questions are harder than changing a portrait on a wall. People have answered them in different ways, some hopeful and some terrible, and Red Threads will look at both.
VISUAL: Quick glimpses of co-operatives, land reforms, a bureaucrat's stamp, a crowd. Each is pinned to the thread.

**[Beat 17 | 4:48]** But a society that only changes its faces will keep getting the same results. / To change what is built, you have to look at what it is built on.
VISUAL: Return to the office door from Beat 1. The camera follows the red thread down through the floor into the foundation, where it starts to pull.
CUT-DOWN: Clip 3 (the closing line).

**[Beat 18 | 5:04]** Next thread: why couldn't the most famous scientist in the world trust elections to stop the rich?
END SCREEN: 20 seconds. The red line runs off the frame into the tile for the Einstein lesson; a red-thread Subscribe knot sits beside it. Nothing else on screen.
SOUND: Underscore resolves; the sting plays once.

**CREDITS:** Lesson by Red Threads · Directed by [animator] · Narrated by [VO] · Music by [composer]

**SOURCES**

- Karl Marx and Frederick Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*, section 1 (Penguin Classics). Beat 10.

**THINK QUESTIONS**

1. What is the difference between the "base" and the "building on top"?
2. Why does Engels look for the causes of political change in the economy rather than in people's ideas?
3. Is corruption a cause or a symptom? Give one argument for each side.
4. Name one change that would reach the base in your country, and one that would only change the surface.

**DISCUSS:** If you could ask any new government one question about the base, what would it be?

## 9. Conversion: from viewer to subscriber

Every piece of content should move a viewer one step along this path: short clip, then full lesson, then subscribe or follow, then the next lesson, then the book. Each step gets one job and one call to action, never more.

### In every long lesson

1. **Win the first 30 seconds.** The title, thumbnail and first frame show the same image and make the same promise. No intro, logo or "subscribe" ask before the hook; most viewers who leave do so in the opening seconds.
2. **Open a loop by 0:30.** Ask the question the lesson will answer and hint at the turn still to come (in the pilot: "And what would it take to change the thing underneath?"), so viewers stay to see it paid off.
3. **Keep the picture moving.** Change the image every 3 to 6 seconds and drop the music before each turn, as the skill specifies. Static frames are where viewers drift away.
4. **One silent subscribe prompt, right after the strongest line.** A 3-second animated red-thread knot with "Subscribe" (or "Follow" on Facebook) in a corner. Never in the first minute, and never spoken.
5. **End on the next question, not a goodbye.** After the final line, the narrator sets up the next lesson in one sentence over a 20-second end screen with only two elements: the next lesson and Subscribe. Don't fade out early, because YouTube end-screen elements can only appear in the last 5 to 20 seconds.
6. **Series playlists.** Group lessons by pillar so autoplay leads to another Red Threads lesson.

### Around every long lesson

- **Pinned comment** with the Discuss question plus the book and page. Reply to comments in the first hours after upload; early replies keep discussion going.
- **Description:** the first two lines restate the hook and link the next lesson; sources go below.
- **Thumbnails:** use YouTube's Test & Compare with three thumbnails per lesson, and carry the winning style into the series.
- **Facebook:** upload natively rather than sharing a YouTube link, add burned-in captions, and share each lesson into relevant groups yourself on day one.

### Reels, Shorts and carousels

- **Hook in the first 1 to 2 seconds** with on-screen text, because many people start with the sound off.
- **End every clip on an open question** that the full lesson answers ("Why doesn't the foundation move? Full lesson on the page.").
- **Link the full lesson everywhere:** as the related video on YouTube Shorts, in bio and in a story link sticker on Instagram, and in the first comment, pinned, on Facebook.
- **Last carousel slide:** "Save this. Full lesson: link in bio."

### What to track after each lesson

- Thumbnail click-through rate on impressions, and the winner of each Test & Compare.
- Share of viewers still watching at 30 seconds, and average percentage viewed.
- End-screen click rate, and subscribers or followers gained per 1,000 views.
- Clicks from Reels and Shorts through to the full lesson.

Change one thing at a time (the hook, the thumbnail or the end screen) so you know what moved the numbers.

## 10. Decision needed: language

With a global audience, the plan recommends English as the master language, with a Bangla version of every lesson. The animation is shared, so a second voice track costs far less than a second video. A native writer should write the Bangla script from the English one and keep each quote faithful. Options:

- **English master plus a Bangla version (recommended).** Global reach from English and a home audience in Bangla. On YouTube, add Bangla as a second audio track on the same video; on Facebook, post the Bangla version natively.
- **English only, with subtitles.** Subtitles in Bangla, Hindi, Spanish and other languages. The cheapest route; subtitles reach viewers who won't hear a dub.
- **Bangla first, English subtitles.** Strongest in Bangladesh, but limits the global reach you want.

## 11. Production workflow per lesson

1. **Pitch** in one sentence: the question, the hook, the source text and page.
2. **Script** in English with the ted-ed-style-explainer skill (550 to 900 words), quotes checked against the PDF, three cut-down beats and the subscribe prompt marked.
3. **Bangla script** written from it by a native writer, then read aloud and timed.
4. **Beat sheet** with narration, visual, transition, on-screen text and sound per beat.
5. **Scratch VO**, then a timed animatic.
6. **Style frames** in the channel palette, three to five per lesson.
7. **Final VO, animation, music and sound.**
8. **Fact check** by someone who has read the source, a check that nothing reads as an attack on the government or a party, and a clarity check by someone new to the topic.
9. **Package:** 16:9 master, three 9:16 cut-downs, captions in Bangla and English, thumbnail, carousel, description with sources, think questions and pinned comment.

## 12. Risks and how to handle them

- **No direct push against the government.** The channel never campaigns against the current government or any past one, never names leaders or parties as the problem, and never calls for protests. Show real leaders, parties or flags only when history requires it, and neutrally. Every lesson is about the base that every government inherits.
- **Partisanship.** The channel loses its argument the moment it looks like it belongs to a party. Name systems, not parties, and avoid party colours and symbols.
- **Legal risk.** Bangladesh's cyber and speech laws have been used against people for online posts. Don't accuse named living people of crimes, keep claims sourced, keep the tone analytical, and get advice from a lawyer who knows these laws before launch.
- **Incitement.** Some source texts discuss revolution. Lessons explain those ideas as history and argument, never as calls to action, protest or violence, which also keeps them within platform rules.
- **Reach limits on political content.** Meta says Instagram and Threads do not proactively recommend political content to people who have not opted in, and YouTube applies advertiser-friendly rules to controversial topics. Frame titles as questions about history and economics, and lean on shares, saves, groups and search.
- **Accuracy.** Quote exactly, link every source, mark inference as inference, and pin a correction when something is wrong.
- **Copyright.** Quote short passages only. Never show scanned pages or the Jacobin book's illustrations. The Foreign Languages Press Engels edition is licensed CC BY-NC-SA (non-commercial), so a monetized channel should quote it, not reproduce it.
- **Fairness.** Give the strongest version of the opposing view, especially in episodes 6 and 9.

## Appendix A. Episode 2 script: Why can't elections stop the rich?

Written with the ted-ed-style-explainer skill. Every quotation is from Einstein's essay as printed in the PDF in Study Materials/Books. This was the first pilot; it now follows the main pilot and reinforces the core idea.

**TITLE OPTIONS:** Why can't elections stop the rich? · Why did Einstein become a socialist? · What Einstein thought was wrong with the economy
**FORMAT:** Standard explainer | **TARGET LENGTH:** about 5 min / 800 words
**HOOK IMAGE:** a printing press in a cramped New York office; a red thread runs off the press and ends in a signature: A. Einstein.

**[Beat 1 | 0:00]** May, 1949. / In a cramped office in New York, a few editors are putting together the first issue of a new magazine. It has no money, no famous name, and very few readers. / But the opening essay is signed by the most famous scientist on Earth.
VISUAL: Pages slide off a press. The red thread rides out on the paper and loops into a signature.
TRANSITION: Push into the signature; the ink lines become Einstein's hair.

**[Beat 2 | 0:14]** Albert Einstein. The man who showed that time can stretch and space can bend. / And his essay's title is a question: *Why Socialism?*
VISUAL: Einstein, drawn simply, at a desk; clocks warp behind him, then settle as he writes the title.

**[Beat 3 | 0:24]** In America in 1949, that was a risky thing to sign. / So why would a physicist, with plenty to lose, put his name to it? What did he think was actually wrong? / And why, by the end, does he warn against his own answer?
VISUAL: Cold War headlines blur past; Einstein's figure stands still among them.
SOUND: Music drops to a single plucked note.
CUT-DOWN: Clip 1 is Beats 1 to 3.

**[Beat 4 | 0:42]** Einstein begins by asking whether he has any right to speak. Economics, he says, is not like astronomy. Most of recorded history belongs to what the economist Thorstein Veblen called "the predatory phase": conquerors seizing the land and making themselves a ruling class.
VISUAL: Planets on clean orbits; then a tangle of threads. Armoured figures plant flags on fields while farmers bow.

**[Beat 5 | 1:04]** Science, he argues, can supply the means to an end, but it cannot choose the end. / So experts are not the only ones with a right to speak about how society is organized.
VISUAL: A scientist's toolbox opens; a compass inside spins, pointing nowhere.

**[Beat 6 | 1:20]** Then he tells a story. A visitor, "intelligent and well-disposed", hears Einstein's fear of another war and calmly asks: / "Why are you so deeply opposed to the disappearance of the human race?"
VISUAL: Two figures in armchairs. The visitor's teacup is perfectly still. Einstein's coffee ripples.

**[Beat 7 | 1:38]** Einstein heard that question as a symptom of "a painful solitude and isolation" that so many people were suffering from. / What causes it?
VISUAL: Pull back to a city of lit windows, each with a single figure inside.

**[Beat 8 | 1:52]** Everything we have, he points out, comes through other people. Society gives us "food, clothing, a home, the tools of work, language, the forms of thought, and most of the content of thought."
VISUAL: One person alone on a blank page; the camera pulls back as red threads run from their coat, their bread and their words to farmers, miners, teachers and the generations before.
CUT-DOWN: Clip 2.

**[Beat 9 | 2:14]** Yet we feel that dependence as a threat. Our selfish drives are encouraged; our social ones wither. We become "prisoners of their own egotism".
VISUAL: The web is still there, but each figure now holds scissors, eyeing the threads.

**[Beat 10 | 2:30]** The source, he argues, is "the economic anarchy of capitalist society": producers trying to deprive each other of the fruits of shared work, "not by force, but on the whole in faithful compliance with legally established rules."
VISUAL: A long shared table of produce; hands pull food toward themselves, each holding a stamped legal document.

**[Beat 11 | 2:52]** A worker, he notes, is not paid the value of what they produce, but according to their minimum needs and how many others are competing for the job.
VISUAL: A baker pulls a whole loaf from the oven and is handed one slice; as the queue of job seekers grows, the slice gets thinner.

**[Beat 12 | 3:08]** Over time, capital gathers into "an oligarchy of private capital", whose power "cannot be effectively checked even by a democratically organized political society." / Why? Because parties are "largely financed or otherwise influenced by private capitalists" who, he wrote, "separate the electorate from the legislature". And the same owners control much of the press, radio and education.
VISUAL: The threads of the web slide toward one knot held by a few hands; the knot pulls on a ballot box, a radio and a school bell.

**[Beat 13 | 3:38]** That's the answer to our question. Elections change who sits in the legislature. They don't change who owns the economy that funds the parties.
VISUAL: Faces rotate through a parliament's seats; the knot beneath the floor never moves.

**[Beat 14 | 3:52]** His remedy: an economy owned by society and planned for need, that guarantees "a livelihood to every man, woman, and child".
VISUAL: The knot loosens; threads spread back across the table, and every place is set.

**[Beat 15 | 4:06]** And then Einstein does something unusual. / He warns against his own answer. "A planned economy is not yet socialism," he writes. It could come with "the complete enslavement of the individual."
VISUAL: Freeze. The planned city tilts; its threads turn into bars.
SOUND: Music cuts to silence before the quote.
SUBSCRIBE PROMPT: after the quote lands, the 3-second red-thread knot appears in a corner, silently.

**[Beat 16 | 4:24]** How do you stop a central bureaucracy from becoming all-powerful? How do you protect the rights of the individual? / He leaves those questions open, at a time when, he wrote, such discussion had "come under a powerful taboo."
VISUAL: The bars bend back into threads; question marks hang on them like knots.

**[Beat 17 | 4:42]** In the end, Einstein's diagnosis was about loneliness as much as money. / "Man can find meaning in life, short and perilous as it is," he wrote, "only through devoting himself to society."
VISUAL: Back to the lit windows. One by one, red threads run between them.

**[Beat 18 | 4:58]** He spent his life searching for the hidden laws that connect the universe. / In this essay, he was looking for the threads that connect us.
VISUAL: The city's threads become a single red line that writes "Red Threads".
CUT-DOWN: Clip 3.

**[Beat 19 | 5:08]** Next thread: the good boss who ran the best mill in Scotland, and still called his workers "slaves at my mercy".
END SCREEN: 20 seconds, next lesson tile and Subscribe knot only.
SOUND: The plucked-string sting.

**SOURCES:** Albert Einstein, "Why Socialism?", *Monthly Review* vol. 1 no. 1, May 1949 (reprinted May 2009); PDF in Study Materials/Books. Beat 3 ("a risky thing to sign") is context, not from the essay: confirm it with an outside source, such as the FBI's released file on Einstein in the FBI Vault, before release.
