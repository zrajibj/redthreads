# TED-Ed Style Animated Explainer

Use this skill to plan, script, storyboard and direct short animated lessons that feel like the TED-Ed YouTube channel ("Lessons Worth Sharing"). It captures the style in general terms. Never copy TED-Ed scripts, characters, artwork, music, logos or the "TED-Ed" name; the output is an original video *in the spirit of* the format, under the user's own brand.

## 1. What defines the style

- **One curious idea per video.** A single question, mystery, story or puzzle, answered completely in 3 to 6 minutes. Depth over breadth.
- **Expert + animator pairing.** Every lesson pairs a subject expert (the "lesson by") with an animation director ("directed by"). The script is accurate enough for a teacher; the visuals are art-directed by a different hand each time. There is no single house look: what is consistent is craft, clarity and metaphor.
- **Narration carries the lesson; animation carries the meaning.** One unseen narrator, no presenter on screen, almost no on-screen text. Every sentence has a picture that makes it easier to understand.
- **Audience:** curious people roughly 12 and up, including teachers using it in class. Smart, never condescending, no jargon without a picture.

## 2. Formats (pick one)

| Format | Shape | Typical title pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Standard explainer | Hook, question, mechanism, twist, payoff | "Why do ___?", "How does ___ work?", "What would happen if ___?" |
| Riddle / puzzle | Second-person scenario with rules, pause point, step-by-step solution | "Can you solve the ___ riddle?" |
| History / biography | Present-tense vignette, then context, then legacy | "The ___ who ___", "The rise and fall of ___" |
| Myth / folklore | Told as a story by the narrator, with a short framing note on its origins | "The myth of ___" |
| Debate / trial | A historical figure is put on trial; prosecution vs defense; viewer decides | "History vs. ___" style (use your own name) |
| "Wacky history of" / "Is it ___?" | Myth-busting a familiar thing, with surprising facts | "The surprising history of ___", "Is ___ actually bad for you?" |

## 3. Script rules

**Length:** 550 to 900 words of narration (about 145 to 160 words per minute). Riddles run shorter, 450 to 700.

**Structure (standard explainer):**
1. **Cold open hook (first 2 to 4 sentences, under 20 seconds).** Drop the viewer into a concrete, vivid moment: a person, a place, a date, a strange observation. Often present tense ("In 1847, a doctor in Vienna notices...") or second person ("You're stranded on an island with..."). No greetings, no "in this video", no channel intro.
2. **The question.** State the puzzle plainly, ideally as a question the viewer now wants answered.
3. **Groundwork.** Explain only the background needed, each idea built from the one before. Use one strong analogy per hard concept.
4. **Mechanism / story escalation.** The meat. Short paragraphs, each one beat that maps to a shot or scene.
5. **Turn or complication.** A twist, counterexample, open question, or "but that's not the whole story" moment.
6. **Payoff.** Answer the question, then widen the lens: why it matters, what is still unknown, a final memorable image or line that echoes the opening.

**Riddle structure:** scenario in second person, stakes, clear rules (each rule its own sentence and visual), a moment for the viewer to try ("Pause now to figure it out yourself; answer in 3, 2, 1" in your own words), then the solution reasoned step by step, including a tempting wrong path and why it fails, then a closing beat that resolves the story.

**Voice and diction:**
- Warm, curious, precise, lightly wry. Humor comes from facts and situations, not jokes.
- Plain words, short-to-medium sentences, active voice. Concrete numbers and specifics ("about 37 trillion cells") over vague claims.
- Speak to "you" freely; ask rhetorical questions sparingly, at the turns.
- Name uncertainty honestly: "scientists still debate", "the records are unclear". Present competing hypotheses fairly.
- No filler, no hype, no clickbait payoff that the video does not deliver.
- Write for the ear: every sentence readable in one breath; avoid parentheticals and long lists.

**Accuracy:** Every factual claim must be checkable. Keep a sources list alongside the script and flag anything inferred. Have a subject expert review.

**Companion material (optional, like the lesson pages):** 3 to 5 "Think" questions, a short "Dig Deeper" paragraph with sources, and one "Discuss" prompt.

## 4. Visual direction

- **Medium:** mostly 2D, digitally illustrated (vector or textured raster) and animated in tools like After Effects, Toon Boom, TVPaint or Animate. Occasional cutout, paper, stop-motion or stylized 3D when it suits the topic. Choose one coherent art direction per video and commit.
- **Illustration:** simplified, graphic characters with strong silhouettes; minimal facial detail but clear expressions; designed shapes over realism. Backgrounds as flat planes with depth by layering and value.
- **Texture:** subtle grain, paper, brush or halftone texture over flat fills, giving a handmade, print-like warmth.
- **Palette:** limited, harmonious palette per video (often 4 to 7 colors plus neutrals), chosen to match the subject's mood: muted earth and parchment for history, deep blues and teals for space/ocean, warm saturated accents for biology. One accent color guides the eye to what matters. Keep strong value contrast for legibility on phones.
- **Visual metaphor is the core tool.** Turn abstractions into objects: a cell as a factory, an economy as a river, memory as a filing room. Diagrams live inside the illustrated world, not as slides.
- **Continuity and transitions:** favor seamless transitions over cuts: match cuts on shape, camera push-ins through an object into the next scene, morphs, zoom from macro to micro, a line that keeps drawing into the next idea, pull-backs that reveal context. Each new narration beat changes the image every 3 to 6 seconds.
- **Motion:** smooth eased camera moves, parallax layers, limited animation on characters (expressive poses, held frames) with fluid secondary motion on elements that explain the idea.
- **On-screen text:** rare. Short labels, dates, names or numbers, hand-lettered or in a clean sans, integrated into the scene. No subtitles burned in (provide captions separately). No bullet lists.
- **Opening:** start straight on the hook image. A tiny brand sting can come after the hook or not at all.
- **Closing card:** credits in the format "Lesson by [expert] · Directed by [animator/studio] · Narrated by [VO]" plus music/sound credits, then an end card with the user's own logo and links to related videos.

## 5. Voiceover

- One narrator for the whole lesson (debate formats may add 2 or 3 character voices).
- Neutral-to-warm, clear, mid-pitch, conversational, like a favorite teacher telling a story. Not announcer-style, not over-acted.
- Pace about 145 to 160 wpm, with real pauses after the hook, before a twist and before the final line.
- Clean studio recording, close mic, no room reverb, gently compressed, peaking around -3 dBFS, dialogue around -16 LUFS for web.
- Mark the script with pause beats (`/`), emphasis (*word*) and pronunciation guides for names and terms.

## 6. Music and sound design

- Original or properly licensed score, composed or cut to picture. Light, curious underscore: plucked strings, marimba, piano, soft synth pads, small-ensemble orchestral textures. Genre shifts to fit the topic (period instruments for history, ambient synth for space).
- Music sits under the voice (about -20 to -24 dB below dialogue) and follows the story arc: gentle intro, builds at the escalation, a drop or silence before the twist, a resolved cadence at the end.
- Sound design is subtle and tactile: whooshes on camera moves, soft pops and clicks as elements appear, a few foley details for characters and settings. Never louder than the voice.
- Short music sting on the end card.

## 7. Titles and thumbnails

- **Title:** a curiosity-provoking question or claim in plain words, under about 60 characters. Patterns: "Why ___?", "How ___?", "What happens if ___?", "Can you solve ___?", "The ___ that ___". Promise only what the video answers.
- **Thumbnail:** a single striking illustrated frame (or a purpose-made illustration in the video's art style), one clear focal subject, bold color contrast, readable at small size. Usually no text; at most 2 to 4 words. No faces of presenters, no arrows or red circles.
- **Description:** a two-sentence summary that restates the hook, then a link to the companion lesson page, the credits, and sources.

## 8. Production workflow

1. **Pitch:** one-sentence question + why it is surprising + who the expert is.
2. **Script:** draft with the expert; tighten to word count; fact-check; read aloud and time it.
3. **Scratch VO + animatic beat sheet:** split the script into beats (one row per 1 to 3 sentences) with columns: narration, visual idea / metaphor, transition into next beat, on-screen text, SFX/music cue.
4. **Style frames:** 3 to 5 key frames defining palette, texture, characters and type.
5. **Storyboard and animatic** timed to the final VO.
6. **Final VO record**, then animation, then music and sound design to picture.
7. **Review** for accuracy (expert), clarity (someone new to the topic) and pace.
8. **Deliver:** 16:9 1080p or 4K master, captions file, thumbnail, title, description, companion questions.

## 9. Output templates

When asked for a script, return:

```
TITLE: (2 to 3 options)
FORMAT: (from section 2) | TARGET LENGTH: x min / y words
HOOK IMAGE: one line

SCRIPT
[Beat 1 | 0:00] narration...
  VISUAL: ...
  TRANSITION: ...
  SOUND: ...
[Beat 2 | 0:12] ...

CREDITS: Lesson by / Directed by / Narrated by / Music by
SOURCES: ...
THINK QUESTIONS (optional): ...
```

When asked for art direction, return: mood words, a hex palette with role of each color, texture choice, character design rules, 3 key metaphors, transition plan, and thumbnail concept.

## 10. Checklist before calling it done

- Hook lands in under 20 seconds with a concrete image, no preamble.
- One central question, clearly answered.
- Every beat has a visual idea; at least three strong visual metaphors.
- Narration within the word target and timed aloud.
- Facts sourced; uncertainty stated honestly.
- Palette limited and consistent; transitions mostly seamless.
- Music never competes with the voice.
- Title and thumbnail promise exactly what the video delivers.
- Nothing copied from TED-Ed; no TED or TED-Ed branding used.
